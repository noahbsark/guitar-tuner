Speaker echo experiment

Find out whether your browser reduces speaker sound picked up by your microphone. This is a diagnostic, not a speaker-monitoring mode.

Setup

Stop any other microphone session. Remove headphones, select your normal speakers, set the device volume low and keep the room quiet. A short, quiet noise sample will play twice. No microphone audio is ever routed to your speakers.

Levels are microphone dBFS estimates after subtracting measured quiet-room noise power, not sound-pressure levels. Results can vary due to room noise, hardware, input gain, browser processing and microphone position. A low echo level does not prove that high-gain live guitar monitoring is safe.

What this tests

The browser is asked to disable or enable its built-in echo cancellation while automatic gain control and noise suppression are requested off. The same locally generated sample plays at a fixed digital level. The microphone is connected only to an analyser; recordings are neither saved nor uploaded.

Echo cancellation is commonly optimized for conversations. It may suppress guitar harmonics, introduce artifacts or fail to prevent feedback when the output is a processed copy of the microphone input.

Offline reference filtering experiment

Try subtracting a known speaker reference from a microphone recording. This experiment uses a short adaptive filter, not the browser's speech echo cancellation. It does not turn on the microphone or enable live speaker monitoring.

For local files, use synchronized recordings from the same session, 2–8 seconds long, with only the speaker reference (no guitar) in the first two seconds. The reference must lead the microphone echo by 0–250 ms. The experiment converts both to mono 16 kHz; the filtered file is for evaluation, not final music production.
